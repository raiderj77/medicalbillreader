## Start by identifying the problem

“Negotiating” can mean several different things: correcting an inaccurate charge, applying for required financial assistance, asking for a published cash price, proposing a settlement, or arranging repayment over time. Those paths use different evidence and have different consequences. Start with the path that fits the bill rather than opening with an arbitrary offer.

This guide provides general information, not a promised strategy or a prediction that a hospital will reduce a balance. No official federal source establishes a standard percentage that people receive merely for asking or paying in one installment. A hospital may correct an error, approve free or discounted care, offer another price, offer financing, or decline a request.

If a term is unfamiliar, use the [medical billing glossary](/glossary). If you used insurance, the companion guide to [reading an EOB line by line](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob) explains how to separate the insurer's notice from the provider's bill.

## Step 1: Reconcile the bill before discussing payment

A hospital bill is not the same as an Explanation of Benefits. The EOB comes from the health plan and shows how a claim was processed, including the billed amount, allowed amount, plan payment, and possible patient responsibility. The hospital bill requests payment from the patient.

When insurance was used:

1. Match the patient, provider, and dates of service.
2. Compare the hospital's balance with the EOB's patient-responsibility amount.
3. Confirm that the claim was processed before treating the provider balance as final.
4. Ask the insurer about any denial, network classification, or amount you do not understand.
5. Ask the hospital about a mismatch rather than paying both documents as though each were a bill.

If the EOB shows a different amount, that does not by itself establish wrongdoing. Claims can be reprocessed, multiple providers can bill separately, and a plan may issue more than one EOB. Preserve the documents and ask both organizations to explain the discrepancy.

The [common medical bill errors guide](/guides/common-medical-bill-errors) covers duplicate charges, dates or services that do not match, and insurance payments that appear not to have been credited.

## Step 2: Request a detailed bill

CMS recommends asking the hospital's billing department for a detailed list of the medical items or services and their costs. This can reveal whether the total represents room charges, facility services, supplies, professional services, or another category.

Use careful wording:

> “Please provide a detailed bill that lists each item or service, its date, billing code if available, charge, adjustment, insurance payment, patient payment, and remaining balance.”

Do not describe this as a universal “federal right to an itemized bill.” CMS advises consumers to request the detail, but the federal hospital-price rule did not create a general itemized-bill entitlement. A state law, health plan, public program, or contract may provide additional rights, so check the rule that actually applies.

Review the detailed bill against what happened, but do not diagnose an error from a code alone. A code is a starting point for a question. Ask the billing office or insurer for a plain-language explanation, and ask the treating provider to review a service description when appropriate.

## Step 3: Ask for financial assistance first

Financial assistance, sometimes called charity care, is not the same as haggling over a price. Tax-exempt hospital organizations subject to Internal Revenue Code section 501(r) must establish a written financial assistance policy for each hospital facility. The policy must explain:

- who is eligible;
- whether assistance includes free or discounted care;
- how the hospital calculates charges;
- how to apply;
- what can happen after nonpayment; and
- which other clinicians providing emergency or medically necessary care at the facility are covered by the policy.

Federal law does not set one nationwide income cutoff for assistance. The hospital's policy defines its criteria, and state law may add requirements. Assistance is not necessarily limited to people without insurance. Read the policy instead of assuming that coverage, employment, or a balance of a particular size makes someone eligible or ineligible.

Start with this script:

> “Before we discuss payment, please send me your current financial assistance policy, plain-language summary, application, and the list showing which clinicians are covered. What is the application deadline, and what happens to billing or collection activity while the application is reviewed?”

That provider list matters. A hospital policy may cover the hospital's charge but not a separately billing physician group. Ask every organization on the bill whether it has its own assistance program.

For emergency and other medically necessary care, a person found eligible under a tax-exempt hospital's policy cannot be charged more than the amounts generally billed to people with insurance for that care. The hospital also may not use gross charges for an eligible person's covered emergency or medically necessary care. This protection follows an eligibility determination; it is not a universal cap for every bill.

Apply before moving the balance to a general or medical credit card when possible. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that using a card can make assistance harder to obtain, even though a person may still ask afterward.

## Step 4: Understand assistance and collection timing

IRS regulations describe a minimum **120-day notification period** and a **240-day application period**, measured from the first post-discharge bill, as part of the reasonable-efforts standard for tax-exempt hospitals. A hospital may accept applications later, and its policy or state law may be more generous.

These rules restrict extraordinary collection actions until the hospital has made required efforts to determine eligibility. They do not mean that every statement, telephone call, or routine billing activity automatically stops when someone asks for an application. Ask what the hospital will do while an application is pending, and keep written proof of submission.

Do not confuse these financial-assistance rules with the collection protections in federal patient-provider dispute resolution. If an eligible uninsured or self-pay person has a bill at least $400 above a written estimate, the [Good Faith Estimate $400 rule guide](/guides/good-faith-estimate-400-rule) explains when PPDR may stop collection of the disputed amount. An assistance application alone does not create that PPDR protection.

## Step 5: Compare relevant prices without inventing a “fair” number

There is no single national fair price for a hospital service. The relevant amount can vary by the service package, setting, location, payer, plan, network status, and patient circumstances.

Useful comparisons include:

- the allowed amount on the EOB;
- the plan's online price-comparison tool for covered care;
- the hospital's published discounted cash price;
- the hospital's payer-specific negotiated charge for the plan; and
- a written Good Faith Estimate for eligible future self-pay care.

Federal hospital price-transparency rules require hospitals to publish gross charges, discounted cash prices, payer-specific negotiated charges, and de-identified minimum and maximum negotiated charges. Those figures are reference points, not automatic promises about a completed bill. Confirm that the code, service package, location, and setting actually match.

You can say:

> “I found the hospital's published price information for what appears to be the same service. Please confirm whether it matches this code, location, and service package, and explain why my charge differs.”

## Step 6: Ask about a cash or self-pay amount

A discounted cash price is a hospital's charge for a person paying cash or a cash equivalent. It is distinct from financial assistance and may have different eligibility and timing rules. Do not promise that asking produces a particular percentage reduction.

After the hospital decides a financial-assistance application, or if assistance is unavailable, ask:

> “Do you offer a discounted cash or self-pay amount for these exact services? If so, does it apply to an existing balance, and can you provide the itemized calculation and all payment terms in writing?”

If insurance was used, ask how accepting a cash arrangement would affect the claim, deductible credit, appeals, and any contract restrictions. Do not cancel or reverse insurance processing based only on a lower-looking number without understanding those effects.

## Step 7: Make a settlement request without a made-up percentage

A provider may consider a lower one-time amount, but no official source supports telling readers that hospitals commonly remove 30%, 50%, or any other fixed share. The balance, policy, age and status of the account, and applicable law can all matter.

A neutral script is:

> “If financial assistance does not apply, is there a lower one-time amount you would accept to resolve this balance? Before I pay, please confirm the amount, deadline, whether it fully settles this account, and what balance will remain afterward.”

Do not send money based only on a verbal statement. Ask for written confirmation that identifies the account, agreed amount, due date, application of the payment, and whether the remaining balance will be zero or forgiven. If a debt collector rather than the hospital controls the account, CFPB specifically recommends obtaining the settlement plan and the collector's promises in writing before paying.

## Step 8: Compare payment plans with credit products

If a valid balance remains, ask whether the hospital offers a provider-administered interest-free plan:

> “Do you offer a payment plan with no interest? Please provide the number and amount of payments, APR, fees, late terms, whether a third-party lender is involved, and whether enrolling affects financial-assistance eligibility.”

“Monthly payments” does not necessarily mean “no interest.” Some products are medical credit cards or third-party financing. Some advertise a promotional period but charge deferred interest if the balance is not paid in full by the deadline or a payment is missed. Fees and credit reporting may also apply.

CFPB recommends asking about assistance before accepting a medical credit card or financing plan. Read the agreement, calculate the total repayment, and distinguish a hospital's own plan from a loan that immediately pays the provider and transfers the debt to a lender.

## Step 9: Keep a clean record

For each call, record the date, department, representative's name, and any reference number. Keep copies of applications and written decisions. Do not place bills, EOBs, account numbers, medical details, or payment information in public posts or ordinary analytics tools.

Before making an agreed payment, confirm in writing:

- the exact amount and due date;
- whether the payment resolves the full account or only one charge;
- whether interest or fees apply;
- what happens to any remaining balance;
- whether insurance processing or assistance changes; and
- who owns the account if a collector or lender is involved.

If the issue is an inaccurate charge rather than affordability, use the correction or dispute path first. The guide to [disputing a medical bill step by step](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill) explains how to organize that request.

## What this process can and cannot establish

These steps can help a person ask the right department for the right option and document the response. They cannot prove that a charge is legally invalid, guarantee assistance, force a particular discount, determine the correct clinical code, or predict a negotiated result.

When a deadline, lawsuit, collection action, or large financial consequence is involved, verify the current federal and state rules and consider help from a qualified consumer attorney, state insurance department, hospital patient advocate, or nonprofit counseling resource.
