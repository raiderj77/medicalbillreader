## Treat the denial notice as the instruction sheet

A denied health-insurance claim is a decision by a health plan, not necessarily a final answer and not the same thing as an incorrect provider bill. The appeal should address the plan's stated reason, use the plan's required process, and arrive before the applicable deadline.

> **Illustrative example notice:** Every name, claim, service, date, plan term, and dollar amount below is synthetic. No example comes from a real patient, promises coverage, or replaces advice from a clinician, attorney, insurer, or regulator.

If a delay could jeopardize someone's life, health, ability to regain function, or control of severe pain, ask the plan and treating professional immediately about an expedited review. Do not postpone medically necessary care solely to work through an online checklist.

The [medical billing glossary](/glossary) can help with terms such as adverse benefit determination, medical necessity, and external review. The [Medical Bill Reader analyzer](/#analyzer) can provide an informational explanation of a document you choose to share; review its current privacy, availability, and pricing disclosures first. It does not decide coverage or file an appeal for you.

## Step 1: Identify what was denied

Start with the EOB and every denial letter. Find:

- The claimant and patient
- Provider and date of service
- Claim or authorization number
- The exact service or amount at issue
- The reason and any denial or remark code
- The plan provision cited
- The internal-appeal deadline and submission instructions
- Any urgent-review or external-review instructions
- Contact information for help or a regulator

The plan may say the service was excluded, out of network, not medically necessary, experimental, not preauthorized, submitted too late, coded inconsistently, or missing information. Those reasons require different evidence. A general letter saying “this is unfair” is less useful than a response to the specific ground in the notice.

First decide whether the issue belongs with the insurer or provider. A provider may need to correct missing or inaccurate claim information. The health plan must review a coverage or benefit decision. Sometimes both need to act, but a provider's claim correction does not automatically preserve the member's appeal deadline.

[How Do You Read an EOB Line by Line in 2026?](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob) explains how to locate the claim decision and patient-responsibility fields. If the disagreement concerns the provider's account rather than the plan's decision, use [How Do You Dispute a Medical Bill in 2026?](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill).

## Step 2: Determine the plan type before relying on a timeline

Appeal rights vary. Ask an employer's human-resources or benefits office whether job-based coverage is **fully insured** or **self-funded**. A familiar insurance company may only administer a self-funded plan, so the logo on the card does not answer the question.

Common categories include:

- Individual or fully insured group coverage regulated partly by a state
- Private-sector employer plans governed by ERISA, including many self-funded plans
- Grandfathered plans with different Affordable Care Act requirements
- Federal, state, or local governmental employee plans
- Church plans
- Medicare, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, VA, or TRICARE coverage with program-specific processes

CMS says the Affordable Care Act's internal-appeal and external-review rules discussed in its general guidance apply to plans or policies created or purchased after March 23, 2010; grandfathered plans can differ. DOL's ERISA claims rules do not govern every public program or governmental and church plan.

Use the notice and plan documents as the controlling instructions. If they are unclear, ask the plan to identify the governing process in writing.

## Step 3: Protect the filing deadline

For many private plans, the internal-appeal filing period is at least **180 days** after the denial notice. DOL states that participants in covered employer plans have at least 180 days, and HealthCare.gov uses a 180-day period in its internal-appeal guidance.

That is not permission to wait. A state rule, public program, contractual procedure, or second-level appeal may have a different deadline. Record the earliest stated deadline and submit in time using the required address, portal, fax, or form.

Keep proof of receipt. If the plan accepts an appeal by telephone for an urgent claim, record when and with whom the request was made, then follow any written-confirmation instructions. DOL guidance says covered ERISA plans must permit urgent appeals orally or in writing.

An informal phone call asking why a claim was denied may not count as a formal appeal. Say clearly that you are appealing and ask for confirmation that the appeal was opened.

## Step 4: Request the claim file and plan language

For ERISA-covered plans, DOL says participants can request relevant documents, records, and other information without charge. Ask for:

- The complete claim and appeal record available to the member
- The plan provision, medical policy, or criteria used
- The meaning of every denial and remark code
- The identity of any medical or vocational expert whose advice was obtained, when applicable
- The internal and external review instructions

Also collect the EOB, provider bill, authorization records, referral, relevant medical records, and correspondence. Send only what supports the issue. Keep originals and submit copies through a verified channel.

If you need a more detailed provider statement to identify the disputed line, follow [How Do You Get an Itemized Hospital Bill in 2026?](/guides/how-to-get-itemized-hospital-bill). That provider request should not delay the appeal deadline; submit what you have and follow the plan's process for supplements when necessary.

## Step 5: Build a focused internal appeal

Organize the packet so a reviewer can see the question quickly:

1. **Cover page:** Member, claim, provider, service date, and contact information required by the plan.
2. **Decision appealed:** Attach the denial or EOB and quote the stated reason accurately.
3. **Requested result:** State whether you want the plan to cover, reprocess, or reconsider the identified item or service.
4. **Reason:** Explain briefly why the cited plan provision, facts, authorization, network information, or medical record supports review.
5. **Evidence:** Attach the specific plan language, provider documentation, authorization, corrected claim information, or other relevant records.
6. **Index:** List attachments and page numbers for a larger packet.

For a denial based on medical judgment, a factual letter from the treating professional may address the plan's criterion, relevant history, alternatives considered, and why the requested service fits the plan's terms. Do not write clinical claims for the professional or exaggerate urgency.

### Synthetic appeal-letter structure

> **Subject: Internal appeal of claim [reference]**
>
> I am appealing the denial dated [date] concerning [service] on [date]. The notice states that the claim was denied because [quote or accurately summarize the stated reason].
>
> I request [coverage, reprocessing, or reconsideration] because [focused factual reason tied to the plan or claim]. The attached documents are [list]. Please include them in the claim record and conduct the full and fair review available under the plan.
>
> Please confirm receipt, identify any missing information, and provide the written determination and applicable external-review instructions. If this request qualifies for expedited treatment, the reason is [facts supplied by the treating professional or plan criteria].

Avoid arguments about a hospital's list price when the plan denied medical necessity; that does not answer the stated reason. Likewise, a provider's assurance that “insurance usually pays” is not a substitute for the plan terms.

## Step 6: Know the common federal review windows

The following are common maximum decision periods in DOL guidance for many ERISA-covered group plans. They are not universal deadlines for every type of coverage.

| Claim type | Initial claim decision | Internal appeal decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urgent care | As soon as medically required, no more than 72 hours | As soon as medically required, no more than 72 hours |
| Pre-service, non-urgent | Generally no more than 15 days | Generally no more than 30 days |
| Post-service | Generally no more than 30 days | Generally no more than 60 days |

Limited extensions and special rules can apply. A plan with two mandatory appeal levels may divide the permitted time between those levels. Some collectively bargained plans have different procedures. The notice and Summary Plan Description should state the process.

Calendar the response date, but do not assume silence means approval. Contact the plan if the deadline passes and preserve the record of every follow-up.

## Step 7: Use external review when the denial qualifies

If the plan upholds the denial, the final internal adverse benefit determination should explain whether and how to request independent external review. External review is not available for every disagreement.

HealthCare.gov identifies common eligible categories including denials involving medical judgment, determinations that treatment is experimental or investigational, and certain rescissions of coverage. Contract exclusions that do not involve medical judgment may follow a different path.

Under the general federal framework described by HealthCare.gov:

- A written external-review request is generally due within **four months** after the applicable final denial notice.
- A standard external review is decided as soon as possible and no later than **45 days** after receipt.
- An expedited external review is decided as soon as medically required and no later than **72 hours** after receipt.

State procedures may meet or exceed federal standards, while other plans use a federal or contracted independent-review process. Use the organization and deadline named in the final denial rather than sending the request to a generic address.

In a qualifying urgent situation, an internal appeal and expedited external review may proceed at the same time. Ask the plan or reviewer immediately; do not infer urgency solely from financial pressure.

## A synthetic denial example

Assume Sam receives an EOB showing that an already-completed imaging claim was denied as “not medically necessary.” Sam does not send a generic complaint to the hospital.

Sam reads the denial, confirms that it is a post-service claim, records the stated filing deadline, and asks the plan for the relevant claim record and medical-necessity policy. The treating professional supplies a factual letter addressing the plan's published criterion. Sam submits the denial, EOB, cited plan language, letter, and a concise appeal before the deadline and keeps proof of receipt.

If the plan upholds the decision, Sam checks whether the final notice permits external review and follows that notice. The example illustrates process only. It does not establish that the service must be covered or that an external reviewer will reverse the denial.

## Where to get outside help

CMS advises people with employer coverage to ask whether the plan is self-funded or fully insured. That answer helps identify the regulator.

- **State-regulated coverage:** A state Consumer Assistance Program or Department of Insurance may explain rights, complaints, and external review.
- **Many private self-funded employer plans:** The U.S. Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration can help with ERISA process questions at **1-866-444-3272**.
- **Medicare, Medicaid, VA, or TRICARE:** Use that program's denial notice and appeal system.
- **Provider account issue:** Contact provider billing while separately preserving the insurer appeal.

California's Department of Insurance, for example, states that it does not regulate self-insured health plans even when an insurance company administers them, and that most private self-funded employer plans fall under ERISA and DOL oversight. That is a useful illustration, not a rule that every self-funded arrangement in every state is ERISA-covered. Governmental and church plans are important exceptions.

CMS maintains a state Consumer Assistance Program directory that points people to a state program, state insurance department, or DOL resource. If deadlines, litigation, or a significant coverage need is involved, consider qualified individual assistance rather than relying only on a general guide.
