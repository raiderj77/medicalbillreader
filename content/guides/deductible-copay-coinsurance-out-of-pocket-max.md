## Four terms explain different parts of your share

A deductible, copay, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum are related, but they are not interchangeable. Each describes a different part of how a health plan divides the allowed cost of covered care between the member and the plan.

The most common source of confusion is the idea that meeting a deductible means insurance will pay everything. Often, meeting the deductible means the plan begins sharing eligible costs. Coinsurance or copays can continue until the member reaches the plan's applicable out-of-pocket limit.

> **Illustrative example notice:** Every plan term, service, and dollar amount below is synthetic. The example is not taken from a patient, customer, medical bill, or insurance claim. Real plan designs and claim calculations vary.

Use the definitions printed in your Summary of Benefits and Coverage, policy, and Explanation of Benefits. The [medical billing glossary](/glossary) provides a starting point, but your plan documents control. If you want help organizing legible bill or EOB fields, the [Medical Bill Reader analyzer](/#analyzer) can provide a first-pass explanation. It cannot confirm coverage, calculate every plan rule, or determine that a charge is wrong.

## The four terms at a glance

| Term | What it describes | Common point of confusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deductible | An amount you may pay for covered services before the plan begins paying for services subject to that deductible | Some services are covered before the deductible, and separate deductibles may apply |
| Copay | A fixed amount for a covered service | Whether it applies before or after the deductible, is waived, or counts toward another limit depends on the plan |
| Coinsurance | A percentage of the allowed amount that the member pays | It can continue after the deductible is met |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | The most the member pays during a plan year for cost sharing that counts toward the applicable limit | Premiums and several other expenses generally do not count |

These calculations normally start with the **allowed amount**, not the provider's full billed charge. HealthCare.gov defines the allowed amount as the maximum payment a plan will make for a covered service. It may also be called an eligible expense, payment allowance, or negotiated rate.

## What is a deductible?

A deductible is an amount a member may owe for covered care before the plan begins paying for services subject to the deductible. If a plan has an annual deductible and the member has not met it, the EOB may assign some or all of an allowed amount to “deductible.”

That does not mean every covered service is treated the same way. Some plans cover specified services before the deductible. A plan can also have separate in-network and out-of-network deductibles, individual and family deductibles, or different rules for prescriptions and medical care.

Look for two figures in the plan portal or EOB: the total deductible and the amount credited so far. Claims can process in a different order from the order in which visits occurred, so the remaining amount may change while claims are pending.

## What is a copay?

A copay is a fixed dollar amount for a covered service. A plan might assign different copays to different kinds of care. The provider may collect one at the visit, but it can still appear as part of patient responsibility on the EOB.

Whether a copay applies before or after the deductible, counts toward the deductible, counts toward the out-of-pocket limit, or is waived under a specific circumstance depends on the plan. Emergency-room benefits sometimes have special provisions, including different treatment if the person is admitted. Do not assume a general example controls your claim.

Save a receipt for any amount collected at the visit. When the provider bill arrives, make sure that payment was credited so you do not pay the same copay twice.

## What is coinsurance?

Coinsurance is the percentage of an allowed amount the member pays for covered care. If a plan applies 20% coinsurance after the deductible, the plan generally pays the other 80% of the eligible amount after any deductible calculation, subject to its other terms.

This is why “deductible met” does not necessarily mean “nothing more to pay.” The deductible phase can end while the coinsurance phase continues. Copays can also continue if the plan applies them. Cost sharing generally ends for eligible covered in-network benefits only after the applicable out-of-pocket limit is reached.

## What is the out-of-pocket maximum?

The out-of-pocket maximum, also called an out-of-pocket limit, caps the cost sharing that counts toward that limit during a plan year. After a member reaches it, the plan generally pays 100% of allowed costs for covered in-network benefits for the rest of that plan year.

It is not a cap on every health-related expense. HealthCare.gov says monthly premiums, noncovered services, out-of-network care, and amounts above the allowed amount generally do not count toward the in-network limit. A plan may also have separate individual, family, in-network, or out-of-network accumulators.

Check which expenses your own plan credits. The accumulator shown in a portal can lag while claims are pending or being reprocessed.

## A synthetic emergency-room example

Assume a fictional plan has these terms:

- $1,500 annual in-network deductible
- $6,500 annual in-network out-of-pocket maximum
- 20% coinsurance after the deductible
- A separate $250 emergency-room copay
- All three kinds of cost sharing in this example count toward the in-network out-of-pocket maximum

Before the fictional ER visit, the member has received credit for **$300** toward the deductible and **$800** toward the out-of-pocket maximum. That leaves **$1,200** of the deductible.

The provider submits a synthetic charge of **$7,000**. The plan's allowed amount is **$4,250**. Under the assumed in-network contract, the **$2,750** difference between the billed charge and allowed amount is an adjustment, not member cost. The fictional benefit treats **$250** as the ER copay and the remaining **$4,000** as subject to the deductible and coinsurance.

Here is the calculation:

1. Allowed amount: **$4,250**
2. ER copay: **$250**
3. Amount subject to deductible and coinsurance: **$4,000**
4. Remaining deductible applied: **$1,200**
5. Amount left after the deductible: $4,000 - $1,200 = **$2,800**
6. Member coinsurance: 20% of $2,800 = **$560**
7. Plan payment: 80% of $2,800 = **$2,240**
8. Total illustrated member responsibility: $250 + $1,200 + $560 = **$2,010**

The totals reconcile: the **$2,010** member share plus the **$2,240** plan payment equals the **$4,250** allowed amount. The adjustment from the provider's original charge is separate.

Because the fictional plan credits the full member share toward its in-network limit, the out-of-pocket accumulator increases from **$800** to **$2,810**. The deductible is now met, but the out-of-pocket maximum is not.

This arithmetic teaches one possible benefit design. A real plan may apply the ER copay differently, waive it after an admission, exempt part of the visit from the deductible, use a different coinsurance rate, or assign separate facility and professional claims. Verify every step against the actual EOB and plan document.

## Deductible met, but coinsurance still applies

Suppose the same fictional member later receives another covered in-network service with a **$1,000** allowed amount. Assume no copay applies to that later service.

The deductible has already been met, so none of the $1,000 is assigned to deductible. But the 20% coinsurance still applies:

- Member coinsurance: 20% of $1,000 = **$200**
- Plan payment: 80% of $1,000 = **$800**

That $200 is not a second deductible. It is the member's percentage under the next phase of the benefit. The member can continue owing eligible copays or coinsurance until reaching the applicable out-of-pocket maximum.

If only $100 remained before that maximum on a later covered in-network claim, the member would generally pay no more than that remaining $100 in eligible cost sharing, and the plan would pay the rest of the covered allowed amount under the assumed terms. Actual accumulators and exclusions still need plan confirmation.

## How the synthetic example maps to an EOB

An EOB is not a bill. It explains how the health plan processed a claim. In the synthetic ER example, its fields might reconcile like this:

| EOB field | Synthetic amount | What to check |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Provider charge | $7,000 | The amount submitted, not automatically what the member owes |
| Allowed amount | $4,250 | The amount used for the assumed in-network calculation |
| Adjustment | $2,750 | The difference between submitted and allowed amounts under the assumed contract |
| Deductible | $1,200 | The remaining deductible assigned to this claim |
| Copay | $250 | Check whether it was already collected |
| Coinsurance | $560 | The member's percentage after the deductible |
| Paid by plan | $2,240 | The plan's share of the allowed amount |
| Patient responsibility | $2,010 | Deductible, copay, and coinsurance combined before subtracting prior payment |

EOB labels vary. Some plans combine fields or split a visit across multiple claim lines. The provider bill can also arrive separately from professional clinicians involved in the visit. The guide to [emergency-room billing](/guides/emergency-room-billing) explains why one episode can generate more than one bill.

Use [How Do You Read an EOB Line by Line in 2026?](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob) to match the patient, provider, service date, claim number, amounts, and remark codes. If the provider bill is higher than the EOB patient balance, ask the provider and plan to reconcile the difference. Also subtract any payment already credited to the account.

## Before paying, verify these six details

1. **Network status:** Confirm the plan's status for each billing provider or facility.
2. **Coverage:** Check whether each service was processed as covered, denied, or pending.
3. **Allowed amount:** Do not calculate coinsurance from the submitted charge unless the plan says that is the correct base.
4. **Accumulator:** Confirm the deductible and out-of-pocket amounts credited before and after the claim.
5. **Prior payments:** Make sure the bill credits every copay or deposit already paid.
6. **Appeal deadline:** If the plan denied or misprocessed the claim, preserve the notice and follow its deadline. The guide to [appealing a health insurance claim denial](/guides/appeal-health-insurance-claim-denial) explains that separate process.

Ask the insurer to walk through the calculation if the numbers do not reconcile. Ask which Summary of Benefits and Coverage provision applies and request a reference number for the call. A clear question about the arithmetic is more useful than assuming that a large bill is automatically incorrect.
