> Educational only. This guide explains how health FSAs generally work. It is not tax, legal, or financial advice, and your own plan's rules control what you can claim and when.

## FSA use it or lose it, in one paragraph

A health flexible spending account (FSA) lets you set aside pre-tax pay for medical costs, but most plans make you spend the money by the end of the plan year or lose it. Paying medical bills you already owe is the cleanest way to use the balance: the care already happened, the expense is documented, and you are not buying anything you do not need. Check your balance and your plan's exact deadline today, then work the list below.

## What use it or lose it actually means

Use it or lose it is the rule that unspent health FSA money is forfeited when the plan year ends, unless your employer chose one of two relief valves. A plan may offer a carryover, which rolls a limited amount into the next year (for 2026, the IRS carryover cap is $680), or a grace period, which gives you up to two and a half extra months to incur new expenses (to March 15 for a calendar-year plan). A plan can offer one or the other, never both, and it is not required to offer either. Your summary plan description, or a two-minute call to your plan administrator, tells you which design you have. Do not guess: the right December move is completely different under each one.

Two more dates matter. The deadline to incur an expense is December 31 for most calendar-year plans, but the deadline to submit claims often runs 60 to 90 days later. And for 2026 planning, the IRS health FSA salary reduction limit is $3,400, per IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32.

## Step 1: Find your real balance and your real deadline

Log in to your plan administrator's portal, not just your payroll stub, and write down three things: your current balance, the last day to incur expenses, and the last day to submit claims. Balances shown in December are often lower than you think because recent card purchases have not settled, or higher because a November claim is still processing. The portal number is the one that counts.

## Step 2: Collect every bill you already owe

Gather the statements from the year: hospital, lab, imaging, urgent care, dental, vision, therapy, prescriptions paid out of pocket. Check patient portals too; small lab and imaging bills hide there. For each bill, note the date of service, because FSA eligibility follows the date the care happened, not the date you pay. Then, before you pay anything, read the bill the way our [guide to reading an EOB](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob) and our [medical bill dispute guide](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill) teach: confirm the service happened, the coding looks right, and insurance processed it first if it was supposed to.

## Step 3: Pay the undisputed bills, dispute the rest in writing

Pay the bills that are correct, oldest first, using your FSA card where it is accepted or by paying and submitting the receipt for reimbursement. For a bill you believe is wrong, do not pay it just to beat the FSA deadline. A wrong bill paid is leverage lost. Dispute it in writing now, keep a copy, and if the amount at stake is significant, consider paying under protest only after the provider confirms in writing that a correction will refund you. If the deadline pressure is the provider's own billing delay, say so in the dispute letter; late-arriving first statements are a known pattern, and administrators hear it every December.

## Step 4: Spend what is left on care you actually need

With the owed bills handled, look at what remains and match it to needs, not to a shopping list. Refill maintenance prescriptions. Book the dental cleaning, eye exam, or physical therapy visits you have been postponing into the weeks before your deadline. Replace worn-out eligible items you genuinely use, like glasses or contact supplies. If a bill looks suspicious while you are gathering paperwork, run it through the [free scan on our homepage](/) before you pay it; a December deadline is exactly when rushed payments on wrong bills happen.

## Your December checklist, in order

1. Confirm three things in the administrator portal: your balance, the last day to incur expenses, and the last day to submit claims. Write all three down.
2. List every bill from this plan year and match each one against its explanation of benefits before paying anything.
3. Pay the bills that are correct, oldest first, and keep the receipts and confirmation numbers together in one folder.
4. Send written disputes for the bills that are wrong today, not after the deadline, and keep a copy of each letter.
5. Book the postponed care you genuinely need, dental, vision, therapy, prescription refills, inside the remaining window.
6. Submit every claim before the submission deadline, then save a screenshot of the final balance and the claim confirmations for your records.

## How to choose where the money goes

- **Bill is correct and owed:** pay it with the FSA. Highest certainty, zero waste.
- **Bill looks wrong and the deadline is weeks away:** dispute in writing first, pay the corrected amount.
- **Bill looks wrong and the deadline is days away:** document the dispute with the provider and the administrator, and decide with both on the phone, not by guessing.
- **No bills owed, balance remains:** schedule real care before the deadline, then eligible staples you will use within months.
- **Balance is small (under about $50):** let it go if using it means buying things you do not need. Forfeiting $40 beats spending $120 to rescue it.

## Frequently confused points

**An HSA is not an FSA.** If you have a health savings account instead, there is no use it or lose it deadline; HSA money rolls over every year. Do not rush-spend an HSA in December.

**Your FSA debit card declining proves nothing.** Cards decline for substantiation and merchant-category reasons even on eligible expenses. Pay another way and submit the receipt.

**You cannot prepay next year's care to drain the account.** Expenses count when the service happens. A deposit on January orthodontics is generally not a December expense.
