## The short answer

There is no single deadline for disputing a medical bill, because "dispute" covers at least four different processes, each with its own clock. A health plan denial usually gives you 180 days to appeal internally. An uninsured or self-pay bill that lands $400 or more above its Good Faith Estimate gives you 120 calendar days to start the federal dispute process. A provider's own billing mistake has no universal federal deadline at all, which is an argument for asking immediately, not for waiting.

The table in the next section puts the main clocks side by side. Every clock runs from a trigger document, such as the date on the first bill or the denial notice, not from the day you happen to open the envelope.

## First, name the problem correctly

The right deadline depends on which of these you are dealing with:

- **The plan denied or underpaid a claim.** That is an appeal, and the plan's deadlines control. The [insurance denial appeal guide](/guides/appeal-health-insurance-claim-denial) walks through the steps.
- **The provider billed something wrong.** That is a correction request to the billing office, covered step by step in the [medical bill dispute guide](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill).
- **You had no insurance and the bill far exceeds the estimate.** That is the federal Patient-Provider Dispute Resolution process, explained in the [Good Faith Estimate guide](/guides/good-faith-estimate-400-rule).
- **The bill is accurate but unaffordable.** That is assistance or negotiation, not a dispute. See the [financial assistance guide](/guides/hospital-financial-assistance-charity-care) and the [negotiation letter guide](/guides/medical-bill-negotiation-letter).

If the lines on the bill are still a blur, Medical Bill Reader's [free bill scan](/#analyzer) organizes a bill or EOB in plain English and suggests questions to verify. Sorting out which problem you have is the step that tells you which clock is running.

If a term in your denial letter or estimate is unfamiliar, the [medical billing glossary](/glossary) defines the ones that carry deadlines with them, including prior authorization and balance billing.

## The main clocks, side by side

| Situation | Clock | Cost to start | Where the rule comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uninsured or self-pay bill is $400 or more above the Good Faith Estimate | 120 calendar days from the date of the first bill | $25 administrative fee | CMS Patient-Provider Dispute Resolution |
| Health plan denied a claim: internal appeal | Generally 180 days from the denial notice | No filing fee | HealthCare.gov appeal guidance and your plan notice |
| External review after a final internal appeal decision | Generally 4 months from the final decision notice | No filing fee in the federal process | HealthCare.gov external review guidance |
| Financial assistance application to a tax-exempt hospital | At least 240 days from the first post-discharge billing statement | No fee | IRS section 501(r) |
| Extraordinary collection actions by a tax-exempt hospital | Generally cannot start until at least 120 days after the first post-discharge billing statement, and only after reasonable efforts to check assistance eligibility | Not applicable | IRS section 501(r)(6) |
| Debt collector sends a validation notice | 30 days to dispute or request validation in writing | No fee | Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, via CFPB guidance |
| Provider billed the wrong thing and you ask for a correction | No single federal deadline; plan contracts and state law can set limits | No fee | Provider policy, plan contract, state law |

## How to read this table

A few sourcing notes, because deadline advice is easy to get wrong:

- **Your documents control.** The general figures above come from CMS, HealthCare.gov, the IRS, and CFPB guidance, linked in the sources. The denial letter, estimate, and statements in your hands state the dates that actually bind you. When a general figure and your notice disagree, follow your notice and ask the issuer to confirm in writing.
- **Clocks start on trigger dates.** The estimate dispute runs from the date of the first bill for the disputed items. The appeal runs from the denial notice. The assistance window runs from the first post-discharge billing statement. Using the wrong start date is the most common way people misjudge a deadline.
- **Calendar days are not business days.** The 120-day federal window counts calendar days, weekends included. If a deadline lands on a weekend or holiday, do not assume it rolls forward; file early.
- **State law can add time or take it away.** State surprise-billing laws, insurance rules, and contract limitation periods vary. Your state insurance department is the authority for its own rules, and the [surprise billing guide](/guides/surprise-medical-bill-no-surprises-act) explains where federal protection stops.

If two clocks could apply, work to the earlier one. Filing the time-sensitive process first preserves options; the slower request can usually follow.

## Worked example: one fictional bill, three different dates

Imagine a self-pay patient who received a Good Faith Estimate of $900 for a procedure, then a first bill dated March 1, 2026 for $1,600. The gap is $700, which is $400 or more above the estimate, so the federal dispute window is open.

- **Federal estimate dispute:** 120 calendar days from March 1, 2026 lands on June 29, 2026. Starting the process by that date, with the $25 fee, is the time-sensitive move, and the disputed amount generally cannot be sent to collections while the review is pending.
- **Financial assistance:** if the provider is a tax-exempt hospital, its assistance application period runs at least 240 days from the first post-discharge billing statement. Counting from March 1, 2026, that reaches October 27, 2026. The assistance review can proceed on its own track while the dispute is open.
- **A correction request:** if one line on the same bill is a duplicate, there is no federal clock for asking the billing office to remove it. The practical limit is different: ask while records are fresh and before the account is sold or reported, and keep proof of when you asked.

One bill, three dates, and the earliest one is not the only one that matters. The dispute protects the amount in the near term; the assistance window can still reduce what is owed months later.

## "The deadline passed, so there is nothing I can do"

Treat a missed deadline as the end of one path, not the end of the problem.

- **Billing offices can still correct genuine errors voluntarily.** A formal deadline limits a process; it does not make a wrong charge right. Send the correction request in writing, with the itemized line and your records, as the [dispute guide](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill) lays out.
- **Assistance windows are long.** The 240-day floor for tax-exempt hospitals outlasts most appeal clocks, and some hospitals accept applications beyond their minimum period.
- **Regulators take complaints on separate tracks.** The CMS No Surprises Help Desk (1-800-985-3059) and your state insurance department accept complaints about billing and claim handling; a complaint is not an appeal, but it creates a record and can prompt a review.
- **A collector's letter restarts a different clock.** If the account has already gone to a debt collector, the 30-day validation window in the collector's notice is a real deadline with real consequences for your rights, so read that notice on the day it arrives.

What a missed deadline does change: you lose the leverage of the formal process, including the collection pause that comes with the federal estimate dispute. That is a reason to calendar dates early, not a reason to stop asking questions.

## What to do this week

1. **Find the trigger documents.** The first bill, the Good Faith Estimate if you received one, the EOB, and any denial letter. The dates printed on them start your clocks.
2. **Compute every date that could apply** using the table above, and write the earliest one on the calendar with a two-week warning ahead of it.
3. **File the time-sensitive process first.** Appeals and the federal estimate dispute have hard edges; a billing-office correction request can usually follow in parallel.
4. **Ask for a written hold.** When you contact the billing office, ask whether the account will be held from collections while your dispute or appeal is pending, and get the answer in writing.
5. **Keep proof of everything.** Save envelopes or screenshots showing dates, copies of what you sent, and delivery receipts. Deadline disputes are won with records.
6. **Recheck the math before you file.** If the amounts on your bill and EOB do not line up and you cannot tell which lines drive the balance, run both documents through the [free bill scan](/#analyzer) so your filing talks about specific lines, dates, and amounts.
