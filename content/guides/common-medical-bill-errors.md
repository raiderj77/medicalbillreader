## Six issues to review, not six conclusions

A medical bill can contain a real mistake, but a surprising line is not proof of an error or wrongdoing. Similar services may be reported more than once, providers may bill separately, and a short description may omit important coding context.

The six patterns below are practical reasons to ask for an explanation or correction. They are not a frequency ranking, a fraud test, or a substitute for review by the provider, health plan, or a qualified billing professional.

> **Illustrative example notice:** All names, dates, services, codes, and amounts in this guide are synthetic. No example comes from a patient or customer bill.

Start with three documents when they apply: the provider's detailed bill, the health plan's explanation of benefits, and proof of any payment you already made. The [medical billing glossary](/glossary) explains terms such as allowed amount, deductible, balance billing, and itemized bill. For help matching EOB fields first, read [How Do You Read an EOB Line by Line in 2026?](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob).

## 1. A possible duplicate charge

### How to spot it

Look for two lines with the same date, provider, description or code, amount, and number of units. Then check whether both lines also appear on the EOB.

A repeated line is not automatically a duplicate. A service may have been performed twice, a laboratory may report multiple units, or a facility and clinician may submit separate claims. Billing modifiers can also distinguish services that look identical in a short description.

CMS advises consumers to check whether they were billed twice and notes that double billing deserves a question, especially when more than one provider was involved. The careful conclusion is “possible duplicate,” not “confirmed duplicate.”

### What to ask

Call the billing department shown on each bill:

> “These two lines appear to have the same service date, description, amount, and units. Please explain what separate service each line represents. If one is a duplicate, please correct the account and send an updated detailed bill.”

Write down the representative's name or reference number and the expected response date. If the health plan processed both lines, ask the plan whether it needs a corrected claim from the provider.

## 2. A service you do not recognize or cannot find in the records

### How to spot it

Compare the date and plain-language description on the bill with your EOB and the existing records you obtained. Watch for a date, medication, supply, or procedure that does not match what you remember or what the records document.

Do not treat a brief billing label as a complete clinical description. A facility fee, bundled service, or legal business name can be unfamiliar without being wrong. Ask the provider to identify what the line represents and where the underlying item or service is documented.

CMS tells consumers to compare medical records with the bill and contact the provider about anything not documented. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau also identifies collection for a service never received as a problem consumers should challenge.

### What to ask

> “I do not recognize the item listed as [description] on [date]. Please identify the service, the rendering provider, and the record that supports the charge. If it was entered on my account in error, please remove it and correct any claim sent to the health plan.”

Keep the request factual. An unexplained line warrants review, but the bill alone does not establish intent.

## 3. A billing code that appears not to match the service

### How to spot it

If the detailed bill shows a CPT, HCPCS, or other procedure code, compare a reliable plain-language explanation with the date and general type of care documented. CMS recommends looking up billing codes and asking the provider when the description does not appear to match the care received.

Do not rely on a code search alone. Modifiers, units, place of service, bundling rules, and documentation can affect how a claim is reported. Some code descriptions are copyrighted, and an informal summary may omit details from the official coding materials.

### What to ask

> “The plain-language information I found for this code does not appear to match the service described in my records. Please have the coding team compare the submitted code, modifiers, units, and place of service with the documentation. Please send me the result in writing.”

The appropriate request is a coding review. Do not demand that an unqualified representative substitute a code you selected online.

## 4. A code that may represent a higher level than the documented service

### How to spot it

Some service families have levels that reflect factors such as complexity or documented work. A high-level code paired with records that appear to describe a more limited service may deserve review.

The HHS Office of Inspector General describes “upcoding” as using codes that reflect a more severe condition or a more expensive service than was provided. That is a serious conclusion. A bill reader, patient, or article cannot establish upcoding from a code and price alone. The documentation, coding rules, and claim context must be reviewed, and a simple coding mistake is not the same as knowing fraud.

### What to ask

> “Please review whether the billed service level is supported by the documentation for this date. I am requesting an explanation and coding review, not alleging fraud. If the code should be corrected, please update both the bill and the insurance claim.”

Use “possible higher-level coding” until a qualified review establishes what happened. Avoid accusing an individual clinician or billing employee.

## 5. A cancellation or no-show fee that conflicts with the policy or your records

### How to spot it

First determine whether the line is a fee for a missed business appointment or a claim for care. Then compare it with the provider's written cancellation policy, the time you canceled, and any confirmation message.

A cancellation fee is not automatically a medical billing error or illegal charge. CMS policy allows physicians and suppliers to charge Medicare beneficiaries directly for missed appointments when the policy and fee apply equally to Medicare and non-Medicare patients. CMS also says missed-appointment fees should not be billed to Medicare. Other plan terms and state laws can differ.

Question the fee when the provider canceled, you canceled within the disclosed period, the fee differs from the written policy, or the office appears to have submitted the no-show fee to insurance as a medical service.

### What to ask

> “This account shows a cancellation or missed-appointment fee. I canceled on [synthetic date/time], and the policy or confirmation I have says [brief fact]. Please review the fee against the policy in effect when I scheduled. Also confirm that this fee was not submitted to insurance as a provided medical service.”

If the fee is consistent with a disclosed policy, you may ask for a courtesy waiver, but do not describe that negotiation as correction of an established billing error.

## 6. An insurance payment, adjustment, or prior patient payment that is not credited

### How to spot it

Compare the provider bill with the EOB's insurer-payment, adjustment, and patient-responsibility fields. Also check receipts or portal history for copays and other payments you made. Look for a provider balance that appears to include an amount already credited elsewhere.

The CFPB identifies bills already paid by the patient or insurer as charges worth challenging. CMS says the provider bill should not be higher than the EOB's patient balance. Still, an insurance payment does not mean that every remaining dollar is wrong. A deductible, copay, coinsurance, noncovered item, lawful out-of-network balance, or separate professional bill may remain.

### What to ask

> “My EOB shows an insurer payment or adjustment of [synthetic amount], and my receipt shows a prior payment of [synthetic amount]. The current bill does not appear to credit it. Please reconcile the account with the plan and my payment record, explain any remaining balance, and send an updated bill.”

If the disagreement is about how the insurer processed the claim rather than the provider's arithmetic, contact the plan and follow the appeal instructions in the EOB or denial notice.

## How to document a review request

For any of the six issues, keep a simple record:

- The provider account number and insurer claim number, stored privately
- The exact line, service date, and amount you questioned
- The factual reason it does not match another document
- The date, phone number, and department you contacted
- A reference number, promised next step, and expected response date
- Copies of correspondence and corrected documents

Do not send original records. Share only what the provider, plan, or authorized agency needs to evaluate the issue. If a routine call does not resolve the mismatch, use the separated provider, insurer, and debt-collector processes in [How Do You Dispute a Medical Bill in 2026?](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill).
