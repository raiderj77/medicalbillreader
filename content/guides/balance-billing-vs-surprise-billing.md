## What is the difference between balance billing and surprise billing?

Balance billing describes what a provider does: it bills a patient for the difference between the provider's charge and the health plan's allowed amount. Surprise billing describes an unexpected version of that problem, usually involving out-of-network care the patient could not reasonably choose or anticipate.

The terms overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A patient can knowingly choose an out-of-network provider and later receive a balance bill that is not a protected surprise bill. Conversely, federal law can prohibit a balance bill when an out-of-network clinician participated in protected emergency care or certain care at an in-network facility.

> **Synthetic example notice:** All people, services, circumstances, and dollar amounts in this guide are fictional. They are simplified examples, not information from a real patient, bill, insurer, or provider.

These rules were reviewed on October 16, 2026. They apply differently based on coverage, facility type, service, notice and consent, and state law. Use the [medical billing glossary](/glossary) for related terms, then confirm the claim with the health plan and provider.

> **Balance billing:** A provider seeks the portion of its billed charge that remains above the amount the health plan recognizes, in addition to any deductible, copay, or coinsurance the patient owes.

> **Surprise billing:** A patient receives an unexpected balance bill for certain out-of-network care, such as emergency services or a protected out-of-network service connected to an in-network facility visit.

## How does balance billing differ from normal cost sharing?

A deductible, copay, or coinsurance is not automatically a balance bill. Those amounts are forms of cost sharing calculated under the health plan. A balance bill is the additional difference between the provider's charge and the plan's allowed amount.

Consider a purely synthetic claim:

- Provider charge: **$1,000**
- Plan allowed amount: **$400**
- Patient coinsurance: **20% of $400, or $80**
- Plan payment in the simplified example: **$320**
- Difference between charge and allowed amount: **$600**

The $80 is ordinary coinsurance. If the provider also asks the patient for the $600 difference, that additional request is balance billing. Whether it is permitted depends on network contracts, federal and state protections, the service, and any valid notice and consent.

The billed charge alone does not show what a patient legally owes. Start with the EOB, plan terms, provider bill, and any applicable protection. [How Do You Read an EOB Line by Line in 2026?](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob) explains where to find the allowed amount and patient responsibility.

## When does federal law prohibit an unexpected balance bill?

For people using most private health coverage, the No Surprises Act generally limits balance billing for three major categories:

1. Most covered emergency services at a hospital emergency department or independent freestanding emergency department.
2. Certain out-of-network non-emergency services connected to a visit at an in-network hospital, hospital outpatient department, or ambulatory surgical center.
3. Covered air ambulance services from an out-of-network air ambulance provider.

In protected circumstances, the patient's share generally cannot be higher than the applicable in-network deductible, copay, or coinsurance. The plan and provider resolve the remaining out-of-network payment without placing the patient in the middle.

This does not mean the patient owes zero. A large in-network deductible can still create a large patient responsibility. The guide [Surprise Medical Bill? What Can You Do in 2026?](/guides/surprise-medical-bill-no-surprises-act) explains how to compare that responsibility with the actual bill.

## Why is the out-of-network anesthesiologist protected?

Imagine a fictional patient schedules surgery with an in-network surgeon at an in-network hospital. The anesthesiologist assigned by the hospital is out of network. The patient did not select that clinician and might not learn the network status until after care.

Federal rules treat anesthesiology as ancillary care. The out-of-network anesthesiologist generally cannot balance bill the patient above protected in-network cost sharing. The provider also cannot use a notice-and-consent form to waive that protection. The same non-waivable category includes services related to emergency medicine, pathology, radiology, and neonatology, as well as services from assistant surgeons, hospitalists, and intensivists.

Assume, only for illustration, that the plan uses **$700** to calculate protected cost sharing and the plan's coinsurance is **20%** after the deductible is met. The illustrated patient share is **$140**. The anesthesiologist cannot simply demand the remaining difference between its fictional billed charge and the plan's protected calculation.

The real EOB controls the actual plan calculation. This example is not a price estimate, a statement about a specific code, or a promise about a particular claim.

## When can notice and consent change the result?

An out-of-network provider may seek a patient's voluntary waiver in limited circumstances involving certain scheduled non-emergency services or qualifying post-stabilization care. A valid waiver can permit out-of-network cost sharing and balance billing that would otherwise be restricted.

The form must be separate from other documents, explain the protection being waived, identify the out-of-network provider or facility, and include a good-faith estimate of the out-of-network charge. Signing is the patient's choice. If the patient declines, the provider may decline to furnish non-emergency or qualifying post-stabilization care, but it cannot hide the waiver in general intake paperwork.

The frequently repeated “72-hour rule” needs context:

- If the service was scheduled at least 72 hours in advance, notice and consent generally must be provided at least 72 hours before the service.
- If it was scheduled within 72 hours, the documents generally must be provided on the day the appointment is made.
- If notice is provided on the day of service, including for qualifying post-stabilization care, it generally must arrive at least three hours before service.

Notice and consent is never permitted for emergency care before stabilization. It is also unavailable for ancillary services, unforeseen urgent needs, or an out-of-network provider's service when no in-network provider can furnish that service at the in-network facility. Air ambulance providers may never ask a patient to waive federal surprise-billing protection.

## When might a balance bill still be allowed?

A balance bill can remain possible in situations outside the federal prohibition. Examples include:

- A patient knowingly chooses certain out-of-network non-emergency care after valid notice and consent.
- Non-emergency services are obtained at an out-of-network facility.
- A service is not a covered benefit under the health plan.
- Ground ambulance service is out of network and no state, local, contractual, or plan protection applies.
- A type of coverage falls outside the No Surprises Act's private-insurance protections.

State law may add protection, and a state or local rule may regulate a ground ambulance rate. Do not treat “outside the federal Act” as equivalent to “the entire bill is collectible.” Check the plan and the regulator or Consumer Assistance Program for the state where care occurred.

## What might you owe in three common situations?

### Protected emergency or facility-based care

You generally owe the applicable in-network cost sharing shown by the health plan, after any correction or appeal. If the bill exceeds the EOB's patient responsibility, ask both sides to reconcile it before paying the difference.

### A validly waived non-emergency service

You may owe out-of-network deductible, copay, or coinsurance plus a balance above the plan's allowed amount. Review the signed notice, estimate, EOB, and final bill together. A valid waiver for one named service does not automatically waive protection for every provider involved.

### A ground ambulance service

Federal No Surprises Act limits generally do not decide the amount. Suppose a fictional ground ambulance charge is **$1,800** and the plan pays **$700**. Do not assume that the remaining **$1,100** is either automatically due or automatically prohibited. Ask about state law, a local regulated rate, plan terms, and whether the claim was processed correctly.

## What should you check before paying a disputed balance?

Start by matching the provider, facility, service date, and patient responsibility across the bill and EOB. Confirm that insurance payments and previous patient payments were credited. Ask the plan whether the service was protected and whether in-network cost sharing was used.

Ask the provider to identify the balance being requested. Is it a deductible, copay, coinsurance, noncovered amount, or the difference between a billed and allowed amount? Those categories lead to different questions.

If a notice-and-consent form is involved, check when it was delivered, whether it was separate, which provider and service it named, whether the service was eligible for waiver, and whether you received a copy. Do not send medical or billing records through an unverified email or upload link.

If the plan denied or misprocessed a protected claim, use its appeal instructions. If the provider appears to be billing above the protected EOB amount, contact the No Surprises Help Desk at **1-800-985-3059**. A state Consumer Assistance Program or Department of Insurance may also help, although jurisdiction varies for self-funded employer plans.
