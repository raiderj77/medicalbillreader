## The short answer

A medical bill negotiation letter is a written request to a provider's billing office asking for a lower balance, a settlement, or more time to pay a bill you accept may be owed. Send it after two checks: confirm the bill matches what actually happened and what your plan processed, and ask about financial assistance before you offer money. Keep the letter to one page, name one specific request, and ask for the answer in writing.

A negotiation letter is not a dispute letter. If a line on the bill looks wrong, dispute that line first using the steps in the [medical bill dispute guide](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill), then negotiate whatever balance remains.

## What a negotiation letter is and is not

A negotiation letter is a payment request between you and the billing office. It can ask for a discounted cash price, a lump-sum settlement, a no-interest payment plan, or a review under the hospital's financial assistance policy. It creates a dated paper trail that a phone call does not.

It is not a legal filing, and it does not pause a deadline by itself. It does not correct a coding error, overturn an insurance denial, or require the hospital to agree. Health plan appeals run on their own clocks, covered in the [insurance denial appeal guide](/guides/appeal-health-insurance-claim-denial), and assistance decisions follow the hospital's written policy, not the strength of your letter.

If a term in this guide is unfamiliar, the [medical billing glossary](/glossary) defines the ones that matter here, including allowed amount, chargemaster, and balance billing.

## Before you write: three checks

**Check the bill before you price it.** Compare the provider's detailed bill with your Explanation of Benefits and your own records of the visit. The [EOB reading guide](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob) shows how the figures relate, and the [common medical bill errors guide](/guides/common-medical-bill-errors) lists the patterns worth a question, such as duplicate lines and services that do not match the visit. If you do not have a line-by-line bill yet, the [itemized bill guide](/guides/how-to-get-itemized-hospital-bill) covers the exact request to make. If the lines are still hard to follow, Medical Bill Reader's [free bill scan](/#analyzer) organizes a bill or EOB in plain English and suggests questions to verify with the provider or plan. It cannot decide that a charge is wrong; it helps you write about specific dates, codes, and amounts instead of a vague total.

**Ask about financial assistance before offering money.** Tax-exempt hospitals must maintain a written financial assistance policy under Internal Revenue Code section 501(r), and CMS [walks through how to apply for financial assistance](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/your-patient-rights/medical-bill-rights/get-help/guides-resources/apply-financial-assistance). Assistance can reduce a balance by more than a negotiation would, and some hospitals will not reopen an assistance review after a payment plan starts. The [hospital financial assistance guide](/guides/hospital-financial-assistance-charity-care) explains who may qualify.

**Decide on one request.** A letter that asks for five things gets a slower answer than a letter that asks for one. Choose the outcome you want most: a lower total, a settlement for a lump sum, or time to pay. You can make a second request later if the first is declined.

## How the example in this guide was built

This guide follows the same sourcing rules as the rest of this site:

- **Primary sources first.** Process points come from CMS patient guidance, the IRS section 501(r) pages, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, linked where each point is used.
- **The hospital's policy controls.** Discounts, settlements, and plan terms are set by each provider. No federal source publishes a standard discount percentage, so this guide does not quote one as typical or expected.
- **The worked example is arithmetic, not a prediction.** One fictional balance is carried through three payment paths so the mechanics are visible. A real hospital can accept, counter, or decline each path.

## What to put in the letter

Keep the letter factual and short. Include:

1. Your full name, the patient name if different, the account number from the statement, and the dates of service.
2. One sentence stating what you are asking for and the exact amount or plan you propose.
3. Two or three factual sentences of context, such as a payment you already made, an assistance application in progress, or the amount you can pay and when.
4. A request for a written response that states the new balance, how it was calculated, and what happens after you pay.
5. Your phone number and mailing address for the reply.

Leave out your full Social Security number, card or bank account numbers, and medical detail the billing office does not need. A billing office needs to identify the account and the request, not your history.

## A template you can adapt

Replace every bracketed item with your own details. The sample balance and offer are fictional.

> [Your full name]
> [Your mailing address]
> [Your phone number]
> [Date]
>
> Billing Department, [Hospital or practice name]
> [Billing address from your statement]
>
> Re: Account [account number], patient [patient name], dates of service [dates]
>
> Dear Billing Department,
>
> I am writing about the balance of $[amount] on the account above. I have reviewed the detailed bill and my Explanation of Benefits, and I understand this is the amount your office shows as my responsibility.
>
> I am asking whether you will accept $[offer amount] as payment in full if I pay within [number] days of your written acceptance. [Alternative sentence for a plan: I am asking to pay the balance in [number] monthly payments of $[amount] with no interest or fees.]
>
> I have also [applied for financial assistance on (date) / asked for your financial assistance application], and I would like that review completed before this account is treated as final.
>
> Please reply in writing with the amount you can accept, how it was calculated, and confirmation of what the balance will be after my payment. Thank you for your time.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your signature and printed name]

CMS publishes a similar plain-language approach in its guidance on [talking to your provider about your bill](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/your-patient-rights/medical-bill-rights/get-help/guides-resources/talk-your-provider-about-bill), including asking what options exist before agreeing to pay.

## Worked example: one fictional $2,400 balance, three paths

The table below carries one fictional self-pay balance of $2,400 through three common paths. The figures are arithmetic on that balance only. They are not typical results, and no hospital owes any of these outcomes.

| Path | What you pay in this example | When it is paid | What must be confirmed in writing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No-interest payment plan | $200 per month, $2,400 total | Finished in month 12 | Monthly amount, no interest or fees, and that applying for assistance stays possible |
| Lump-sum settlement offer | $1,200 once | Within days of written acceptance | That the payment closes the account in full and no balance remains |
| Assistance approval at 50 percent, then a plan | $1,200 balance, paid at $100 per month | Finished in month 12 | The assistance decision letter and the adjusted balance it produced |

How to read the three paths:

- **The plan** is best for a balance you can genuinely afford over time. It is not a saving; you pay every dollar. It is not for a balance that might qualify for assistance, because assistance should be decided first.
- **The lump-sum offer** is best when you have savings you can use without draining money you need for housing and essentials. It is not for an offer you cannot actually pay on the stated schedule; a missed settlement can be withdrawn.
- **The assistance path** is best when your income may fit the hospital's policy. It is not a negotiation at all; the policy decides, and the answer comes as a decision letter rather than a counter-offer.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance on [what to do if you cannot pay a medical bill](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-i-cant-pay-a-medical-bill-en-2125/) makes the same ordering point: ask about assistance and payment options before borrowing to pay.

## "If I offer less, will they send me to collections faster?"

This is the worry that stops most people from writing. Asking for a discount, a settlement, or a plan does not change the amount you owe or hand the hospital a new collection tool. Collection activity follows the provider's own policies and the law that applies to it.

For tax-exempt hospitals, the IRS [billing and collections rules under section 501(r)(6)](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/billing-and-collections-section-501r6) limit extraordinary collection actions, such as most credit reporting and lawsuits, until the hospital has made reasonable efforts to determine whether the patient qualifies for financial assistance. Those rules also set minimum windows, including an assistance application period that runs at least 240 days from the first post-discharge billing statement. For-profit providers and physician groups are not covered by 501(r), so ask any provider what its own collection timeline is and note the answer with the date and the name of the person who gave it.

Two habits protect you either way. Send the letter in a way that proves delivery, and keep paying nothing on a verbal promise: if a settlement or plan is offered, get the terms in writing before money moves, as the [hospital bill negotiation guide](/guides/how-to-negotiate-a-hospital-bill) explains step by step.

## How to choose: call, letter, or both

- **Call only** when the question is small and factual, such as which address or code a line refers to. Note the date, time, and name of the representative.
- **Letter only** when you are making a settlement offer or asking for a decision you need documented, and no deadline is close.
- **Call, then letter** for most larger balances. The call tells you which options the office actually offers; the letter makes the request and records the answer.
- **Call the same day** when a statement mentions a deadline, a collection placement, or a discount that expires. Follow up with the letter the same week so the conversation is on paper.

If writing the letter means first untangling which lines the balance is made of, run the statement through the [free bill scan](/#analyzer) and write from the organized version. Specific dates and amounts get specific answers.

## Sending and follow-up checklist

1. Print or save a complete copy of the signed letter.
2. Mail it to the billing address on the statement. USPS Certified Mail with Return Receipt Requested gives you a delivery record; keep the receipt with your copy.
3. Calendar a follow-up 30 days after delivery. If there is no reply, call, reference the letter's date, and send a second copy.
4. When an agreement arrives, compare it with your request line by line before paying: amount, deadline, and what the remaining balance will be.
5. After paying, keep the agreement and proof of payment with the bill and EOB. Records matter if the account is later sold or reported in error, and the [dispute deadlines guide](/guides/how-long-to-dispute-a-medical-bill) explains the clocks that can still apply.
