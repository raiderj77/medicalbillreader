## First, an EOB is not a bill

An **explanation of benefits**, usually shortened to EOB, is a claim summary from your health plan. It shows how the plan processed care that a provider reported. It is not a request for payment.

That distinction matters. The large number under “patient responsibility” may look like an invoice total, but you should not send payment to the health plan just because it appears on the EOB. A provider or facility normally sends a separate bill. The EOB is the document you use to check that later bill.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) explains that an EOB helps you see the total charges, how much the plan covers, and what you may owe when the provider sends a bill. An EOB can also reveal a service, date, network status, or amount that deserves a question.

> **Illustrative example notice:** Every name, service, date, and dollar amount below is synthetic. It is not taken from a patient, customer, or real medical bill.

EOB designs vary. Your plan may use “negotiated amount” instead of “allowed amount,” or “your share” instead of “patient responsibility.” Use the definitions printed on your EOB and the terms in your own plan documents. The [medical billing glossary](/glossary) can help you decode unfamiliar terms, but your plan controls how it processes your claim.

## Read the identification section first

Before doing any math, make sure the EOB belongs to the correct claim.

### Patient and member information

Confirm the patient name and, where shown, the member or subscriber. A family policy can generate EOBs for more than one covered person. Do not rely on the dollar amount alone to identify the claim.

### Provider and date of service

Look for the provider or facility and the date care was reported. A provider name may be the legal name of a medical group rather than the name you saw on the office door. That difference is not automatically an error, but it is reasonable to call the plan if you cannot connect the name with your visit.

The date should correspond to when the item or service was provided. A range of dates may appear for a hospital stay or course of care.

### Claim number

The claim number is the plan's reference for that submission. Keep it available when you call the insurer. A provider account number and an insurer claim number are different references, so a billing representative may ask for both.

### Plan contact information

Find the member-services phone number on the EOB or insurance card. Use a verified number from one of those documents, not an unexpected text or email. CMS recommends contacting the plan when you have questions about coverage or a claim.

## Work through each claim line

An EOB may contain one line or many. Review each line instead of checking only the total.

### 1. Service date and description

The description is a short label for the reported service, such as an office visit or laboratory test. It may be broad and may not match the everyday words a clinician used. Check whether the date and general description make sense together.

If the EOB lists a service you do not recognize, do not immediately conclude that anyone acted improperly. Ask the plan what code and provider were submitted, then ask the provider to explain the corresponding record. The guide to [six medical bill issues worth reviewing](/guides/common-medical-bill-errors) explains how to document that question.

### 2. Provider charge or billed amount

This is the amount the provider submitted to the health plan. It is not necessarily the amount the plan recognizes, the amount the provider will receive, or the amount you owe.

A high billed amount can be startling. On its own, however, it does not show what the claim will cost you. Continue across the line to the allowed amount, adjustments, insurer payment, and patient responsibility.

### 3. Allowed amount

The allowed amount is the maximum amount the plan recognizes for a covered service under the plan's rules. CMS also describes it as the amount the provider will be paid for the claim line. It may be called an eligible expense, payment allowance, or negotiated rate.

For in-network care, the difference between the billed amount and allowed amount is often shown as a contractual adjustment. For out-of-network or noncovered care, different rules can apply, and a provider may sometimes seek an additional balance. Federal and state surprise-billing protections may limit that practice in specific situations; they do not apply to every out-of-network bill.

### 4. Deductible

A deductible is an amount you may have to pay for covered services before the plan begins paying for services subject to that deductible. Some services can be covered before the deductible, and plans can have separate deductibles. The EOB should show how much of this claim the plan applied to your deductible.

### 5. Copayment

A copayment is a fixed amount for a covered service, often collected at the visit. If you already paid it, the EOB may still display it as part of your cost sharing. Check the provider bill or receipt so you do not pay the same copay twice.

### 6. Coinsurance

Coinsurance is a percentage of the allowed amount that you pay for covered care. It is different from a copay, which is a fixed amount. Your plan may calculate coinsurance only after an applicable deductible has been met.

### 7. Paid by insurer

This is what the plan says it paid or will pay the provider. A zero does not by itself mean the entire billed charge is yours. The claim might have been applied to a deductible, denied, sent back for information, or reduced by an adjustment. Read the claim status and remark codes.

### 8. Patient responsibility or “what you owe”

This is the plan's calculation of your share after processing. It can include a deductible, copay, coinsurance, or an amount the plan says is not covered.

CMS cautions that the EOB may not know what you already paid. Treat patient responsibility as a comparison point, not proof of the provider's current account balance.

### 9. Remark and denial codes

A remark code explains why the plan handled a line a certain way. The definition may appear at the bottom or on another page. Read it before assuming “not paid” means “you owe the provider.” If the explanation is unclear, ask member services to translate it and identify any deadline for an appeal.

## A synthetic deductible and coinsurance example

Assume a plan recognizes a synthetic office service at an allowed amount of **$200**. The member has **$50** remaining on a deductible, and the plan then applies **20% coinsurance** to the amount left after that deductible.

1. Allowed amount: $200
2. Remaining deductible applied: $50
3. Amount left for coinsurance: $200 - $50 = $150
4. Member coinsurance: 20% of $150 = $30
5. Illustrated patient responsibility: $50 + $30 = **$80**
6. Illustrated plan payment: $200 - $80 = **$120**

This example teaches the arithmetic; it does not predict any real claim. A real plan may cover a service before the deductible, use a copay, apply a different percentage, deny the claim, or process multiple claims in an order that changes the remaining deductible. Check the EOB and the plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage.

## What should you actually pay?

Pay a provider or facility only from a bill or verified account statement, not from the EOB itself. Before paying, compare the provider bill with the EOB line by line.

CMS says the provider bill should not be higher than the EOB's patient balance. If it is, contact the provider. Also call the plan when the difference involves coverage, network status, an insurer payment, or a claim decision.

There can be legitimate reasons for receiving more than one bill. For example, a facility and a professional may bill separately. You may also receive separate EOBs for different providers or dates. Match each bill to its own provider, service date, and claim rather than adding unrelated documents together.

Do not ignore a due date while a difference is being reviewed. Ask the billing office whether it will place the account under review and how it will handle billing or collection activity. Get a reference number and keep notes. A routine question to a provider does not automatically create a federal collection pause.

## Five checks before paying the provider bill

1. **Identity check:** Confirm the patient, provider or facility, and every date of service.
2. **Service check:** Match each description with care you recognize. Ask for an explanation of unfamiliar lines.
3. **Amount check:** Compare billed amount, allowed amount, adjustments, plan payment, and patient responsibility.
4. **Payment check:** Make sure the provider bill credits insurance payments and anything you already paid.
5. **Status check:** Read every remark or denial code. If something differs, contact the correct party promptly and ask how the due date will be handled.

If the provider cannot explain or correct a mismatch, follow the documented process in [How Do You Dispute a Medical Bill in 2026?](/guides/how-to-dispute-a-medical-bill). If the issue is an insurer's coverage decision, follow the appeal instructions and deadline in the EOB or denial notice rather than sending only a general complaint to the provider.
