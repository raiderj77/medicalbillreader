## Start by choosing the right dispute path

“Dispute a medical bill” can describe three different processes. Using the wrong one can waste time or miss a deadline.

1. **Provider billing review:** Use this for a duplicate line, unexplained service, missing payment, or calculation problem on the provider's account.
2. **Health-plan appeal:** Use this when the insurer denied coverage, assigned the wrong network status, or otherwise made a benefit decision you want reviewed.
3. **Debt-collector dispute:** Use this when a third-party collector contacts you. Federal validation and collection rules can apply that do not apply to an ordinary provider billing office.

You may need more than one path. For example, a provider may need to correct a claim while the health plan separately reviews an adverse benefit decision.

> **Illustrative example notice:** Every account number, claim number, date, service, and dollar amount below is synthetic. The scripts and letter are general educational templates, not legal advice or a promise of a particular outcome.

The [medical billing glossary](/glossary) explains terms used in this process. If you have not yet matched the claim fields, start with [How Do You Read an EOB Line by Line in 2026?](/guides/how-to-read-an-eob).

## Step 1: Gather the documents without sending originals

Collect the documents that apply:

- The provider or facility bill
- The health plan's explanation of benefits and any denial notice
- A detailed bill, if one is already available
- Proof of copays, deposits, or other payments
- Relevant written estimates, network confirmations, or cancellation records
- Notes from earlier calls and correspondence

Use the provider account number when speaking with the provider and the insurer claim number when speaking with the plan. Keep the documents private. Send copies, not originals, and share only the records needed for the review.

Make one sentence that defines the issue. For example: “The provider bill does not credit the insurer payment shown on the EOB,” or “The health plan classified the emergency physician as out of network.” A focused statement is easier to investigate than “the whole bill looks wrong.”

## Step 2: Ask for a detailed bill and existing billing records

CMS advises consumers to ask the provider's billing department for a detailed list of each item or service and its cost. That request is useful because a summary balance may not reveal the disputed line.

Avoid saying that every patient has an unlimited federal right to force every provider to create a new itemized bill. The verified rule is narrower:

- The HIPAA Privacy Rule generally lets an individual inspect and obtain copies of existing billing, payment, and claims records held in a designated record set by a HIPAA-covered provider or health plan, subject to limited exceptions.
- HHS says HIPAA does not require a covered entity to create a new explanation, analysis, or record that does not already exist.
- Medicare beneficiaries have a separate federal right to request an itemized statement in writing for an item or service for which Medicare paid. CMS guidance gives the provider 30 days to furnish that statement.
- State law may provide additional itemized-bill rights.

Safe request:

> “Please send me the detailed bill you maintain for this account, including each item or service, date, charge, payment, and adjustment. If additional billing or claims records are maintained for this account, please tell me how to request access to those existing records.”

## Step 3: Compare each disputed line with the EOB

Review the service date, description, billed charge, allowed amount, insurer payment, adjustment, prior patient payment, and patient responsibility. The provider bill and EOB serve different purposes, but they should reconcile.

CMS says a provider bill should not be higher than the patient balance shown on the EOB. If it is, ask why before paying the disputed difference. The EOB may not reflect money you already paid, so include receipts in your comparison.

For a methodical review, use [What Are 6 Common Medical Bill Errors in 2026?](/guides/common-medical-bill-errors). Treat a mismatch as a question to document, not proof of fraud.

## Step 4: Call the provider about its bill

Use the number on a verified bill or the provider's official website. Ask for patient billing, not a clinical department.

### Provider billing script

> “I'm calling about the bill for care on [date]. Line [description or code] shows [amount], but my [EOB, record, or payment confirmation] shows [specific difference]. Please explain the charge and review that line. If it needs correction, please send a corrected detailed bill and submit any necessary correction to the health plan. Can you tell me whether normal billing or collection activity can be paused during your review? Please give me a reference number and expected response date.”

Ask rather than assert that collections must stop. A routine provider dispute does not automatically trigger a general federal collection pause. If the office agrees to hold the account, ask for written confirmation.

If the representative cannot explain a code, ask for review by the coding or claim-correction team. Do not tell the office which code to use unless a qualified professional has reviewed the documentation.

## Step 5: Call the health plan about claim processing

Use the member-services number on the EOB or insurance card. Ask the plan, rather than the provider, to explain an allowed amount, remark code, denial, network classification, or patient-responsibility calculation.

### Insurer script

> “I'm calling about claim [claim number] for care on [date]. Please explain the allowed amount, plan payment, patient responsibility, network status, and every remark or denial code. If any part is an adverse benefit decision, please tell me the deadline and exact process for an internal appeal and where to send supporting documents. Please provide the explanation and appeal instructions in writing.”

Federal guidance says insurers must explain claim denials and tell members how to dispute them. Internal and external review procedures vary by plan type. Follow the deadline and submission method in the denial notice.

For many private-sector employer plans governed by ERISA, the U.S. Department of Labor says participants have at least 180 days to appeal a denied claim. Those plans must provide relevant claim records for free on request. Government, church, Medicare, Medicaid, and other plan types can follow different systems, so do not apply the ERISA rule to every plan.

## Step 6: Put the correct request in writing

A written request creates a clear record even when a phone call started the review. State the exact line or claim decision, the factual reason for the disagreement, and the correction or explanation requested.

### Provider dispute letter template

> **Subject: Request for review of bill or account [reference]**
>
> I am disputing [specific line or amount] on the bill dated [date] for service on [date]. My reason is: [brief factual explanation].
>
> I request that you [explain the charge, correct the bill, credit the payment, or submit a corrected claim]. Enclosed are copies of [bill, EOB, payment record, cancellation confirmation, or relevant record].
>
> Please respond in writing by [reasonable date] and tell me how the account will be handled while the review is pending.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name and safe contact information]

Keep a copy. A trackable delivery method can help prove receipt; certified mail with return receipt is one option. It does not by itself create new legal rights or guarantee that the provider will pause billing.

An insurer appeal should instead follow the plan's required form, address, deadline, and evidence instructions. Label it as an appeal when that is what the denial notice requires.

## Step 7: Track the response and protect deadlines

Record the date, department, representative or reference number, promised action, and expected response date. Save corrected bills and revised EOBs rather than relying on a verbal assurance.

Do not let an informal provider review cause you to miss an insurer appeal deadline or a debt-collector validation deadline. Ask each organization which process and deadline applies. If you pay an undisputed portion, identify in writing what the payment covers and retain proof; rules about partial payment and settlements can vary.

## If a debt collector contacts you

A third-party debt-collector dispute is not the same as the provider letter above. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says a validation notice generally tells you the creditor, amount, and how to dispute the debt.

After receiving validation information, you generally have 30 days to dispute all or part of the debt. If you submit the dispute in writing within that period, the collector must stop collection activity until it provides verification. Keep a copy and proof of delivery; CFPB says certified mail is generally a good way to document receipt.

Do not send original documents. If you already paid, send copies of payment confirmation after verifying that the collector is legitimate. CFPB provides dedicated sample letters and a complaint process for debt-collection problems.

These debt-collector protections should not be rewritten as “any medical dispute freezes collections.” That would be broader than the verified rule.

## Where to escalate the unresolved issue

Choose the office by problem type:

- **Provider calculation, coding, or missing credit:** Provider billing office, coding review team, or hospital patient advocate.
- **Health-plan claim decision:** Internal appeal, then the applicable external review described in the denial notice.
- **State-regulated insurance issue:** Your state's Consumer Assistance Program or Department of Insurance. CMS maintains a state directory.
- **Private-sector employer plan process problem:** The U.S. Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration at **1-866-444-3272**.
- **Possible deceptive business practice:** The state attorney general or consumer-protection office may accept a complaint. It is not the default appeal route for every medical bill.
- **Debt-collection conduct:** CFPB's debt-dispute and complaint resources.
- **Medicare, Medicaid, VA, or TRICARE issue:** The applicable program's appeal or assistance route.

CMS's No Surprises Help Desk is for questions and complaints within the No Surprises Act, not a universal coding or billing court.

## When the No Surprises Act may apply

The federal No Surprises Act took effect on **January 1, 2022**. For people with most private health coverage, it generally limits certain unexpected out-of-network bills involving:

- Emergency services
- Certain non-emergency services connected to a visit at an in-network hospital, hospital outpatient department, or ambulatory surgical center
- Covered air-ambulance services

The protections do not apply to every setting, plan, or out-of-network service. Notice-and-consent rules can matter for some non-emergency and post-stabilization care. A stand-alone doctor's office that is not a hospital outpatient department is not one of the listed in-network facility settings.

**Ground ambulance services generally are not covered by the federal No Surprises Act.** A state law may provide additional protection.

If you think a provider, facility, or insurer did not follow the federal surprise-billing rules, call the CMS No Surprises Help Desk at **1-800-985-3059** or use CMS's official complaint form. CMS says the Help Desk can review issues within its jurisdiction and refer a complaint to another federal or state enforcement authority when appropriate.

Do not describe an unmet deductible as a No Surprises Act violation. CMS specifically notes that owing a deductible is not, by itself, a violation.
